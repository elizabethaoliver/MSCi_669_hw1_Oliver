Final Project - Brief Summary

####Questions: 1. How does the presence of the fiddler crab, Minuca pugnax, effect salt marsh stability: resistance to erosion (quantified by soil strength as a proxy) and vertical accretion? 2. Can the ribbed mussel, Guekensia demissa, offset the predicted negative effects of bioturbation of M. pugnax on marsh stability?

####Hypotheses: 1.I predict that the bioturbative effects of M.pugnax will decrease soil strength and vertical sediment accretion. Bioturbation from the fiddler crabs will reduce belowground biomass, bulk density, and aboveground biomass contributing to a reduction in root/plant-mediated sediment trapping.

2.There are known positive effect mussels can have on plant biomass and sediment accretion in salt marshes, I hypothesize they will offset the predictive negative effects of M.pugnax for soil strength, sediment accretion and known negative effects on Spartina alterniflora aboveground biomass. Their presence can act as a mediator for sediment trapping, replacing lost sediment trapping from salt marsh grass reduction and increase soil strength through the presence of their byssal threads.

####Quick Experimental Design Background: In Plum Island, MA where the fiddler crab, M.pugnax is non-native to, I completed a 3-month caging study with 5 treatments: control, reference plot, crab only, mussel only, crab and mussel plot. The study was completed in a randomized block design with 8 replicates. Organisms were added to plots without fiddler crabs to minimize legacy effects and reflect a colonization event. Densities were held at 18 mussels m^-2 which reflect natural densities found throughout Plum Island. Fiddler crab densities were brought up to 85 crabs m^-2 then maintained between 50-85 m^-2 which are similar to intermediate densities found in their native range.The reference plots were the only “cages” that only had the posts and no mesh to determine if the cages themselves influenced the outcome of the results. Bioturbation is not being directly measured in this experiment, however, burrow count is a way to define burrowing activity. It is assumed that burrowing activity is continuously happening when fiddler crabs are present.

####Data collected: All data was collected at the end of the 3-month caging experiment except for live and dead stem count which was taken at the beginning and end of the experimental period. All samples were collected in a 1/16th m^2 plot within the cage at the end of the experiment. Soil strength used, to define erosion potential was taken at 10 cm increments to a 50 cm depth. Sediment accretion which is a factor of vertical accretion was gained by placing sediment accretion tubes for a known time thus a known mineral mass/area/time can be calculated for each treatment. An RTK was used to gain elevation (m). Mussel and crab densities were counted throughout the summer to maintain densities and gained a final 1/16th m^2 count at the end of the experiment. A ~10% sub sample of mussel height (cm) and crab carapace width (cm) were taken before placing organisms in the cages.
